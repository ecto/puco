Why does Lakewood keep losing power?

State regulators' staff found old equipment failing in the heat, not storms.12 The company that owns that equipment is the one that bought a law. Here is how FirstEnergy paid for House Bill 6, how the scheme came apart, and what is being decided about Lakewood's grid now.

The short version

  • The bribe. Between 2017 and 2020, FirstEnergy routed about $60 million to a dark-money group controlled by Larry Householder, who became Ohio's House Speaker in January 2019,6 and paid $4.3 million to a company owned by the man who became the state's top utility regulator.7 It got House Bill 6, which a federal appeals court described as a $1.3 billion bailout: subsidies for two nuclear plants run by its generation subsidiary, plus guaranteed revenue for its three Ohio utilities, billed to ratepayers.8
  • The fall. The FBI arrested the Speaker in 2020.9 He is serving 20 years,10 and FirstEnergy admitted the bribery and paid a $230 million penalty.11
  • The money. The nuclear subsidy, the largest piece of the bailout, was repealed in 2021 before customers paid it.12 The coal-plant subsidy was not: Ohioans paid about half a billion dollars for it before it ended in 2025.13 Separately, after PUCO found that FirstEnergy's Ohio utilities had broken the law,14 $249 million in restitution was credited to their customers in early 2026, about $66 for a home using 1,000 kilowatt-hours a month.15
  • Lakewood. Lakewood's power comes from The Illuminating Company, a FirstEnergy utility known in regulatory filings as CEI.16 Its equipment failed in the heat in 2025 and again in 2026. Both times the staff of the Public Utilities Commission of Ohio (PUCO), the state's utility regulator, sent CEI a notice of probable non-compliance with state requirements for adequate service. Staff said much of the equipment seemed older than elsewhere in CEI's territory. The first notice ended with a repair plan and no fine.17 The second proposes a $3.05 million fine, which is not final.5 No court or regulator has tied the outages to the bribery scheme; the link is the same parent company and the same regulator.
  • What's next. FirstEnergy's CEO said in August 2026 that Lakewood's grid has to be replaced, a job he said will take seven to nine years.18 Two PUCO proceedings will shape how fast that happens and who pays: the pending enforcement notice, which proposes repair deadlines,5 and CEI's rate case, with a hearing beginning March 1, 2027 and local public hearings not yet scheduled.19

Background

Before the bribe

HB6 did not come out of nowhere. By 2016 FirstEnergy had a money problem, and it had already tried the regulators and Washington.

FirstEnergy is a holding company. One part runs the wires, including The Illuminating Company (CEI), which serves Lakewood. Another part, FirstEnergy Solutions, owned power plants, among them the Davis-Besse and Perry nuclear plants on Lake Erie. Wires earn a regulated return. Power plants have to sell into an open market, and by the mid-2010s that market paid less than the nuclear plants cost to run. A federal appeals court later summed up the company's position in 2016 as "dire financial straits." FirstEnergy Solutions was "bleeding cash," and the company wanted a "guaranteed payment" to keep its plants going.20

It tried other routes first. In March 2016 Ohio regulators approved eight-year deals that would have had FirstEnergy's own customers guarantee its plants' income. A month later federal regulators ruled the deals could not take effect without passing a federal review, and they never went forward.21 PUCO then let FirstEnergy add a new charge to electric bills, the Distribution Modernization Rider, set at $204 million for 2017 and $168 million a year for 2018 and 2019. PUCO's stated aim was to support FirstEnergy's credit rating. The Ohio Supreme Court threw the charge out in June 2019 because nothing required the money to be spent on the grid. Customers did not get back what they had already paid.22 A push for federal help went nowhere.20

On March 31, 2018, FirstEnergy Solutions filed for bankruptcy. Days earlier it had told PJM, the regional grid operator, it would close or sell its three nuclear plants.23 FirstEnergy Solutions left bankruptcy in February 2020 as a separate company, Energy Harbor, which kept the plants.24 It had also failed to get a bailout from the Ohio House Speaker at the time. What it needed was a different Speaker.20

By then, Larry Householder had spent more than a year working to become that Speaker. He had been Speaker in the early 2000s, spent a decade out of office, and was elected to the Ohio House again in November 2016. According to the trial evidence as summarized by the federal appeals court in Householder's case, that month he ran into FirstEnergy CEO Chuck Jones at Game 7 of the World Series in Cleveland, and they talked about the company's urgent need for help. Two months later they met again over dinners in Washington, with FirstEnergy's Michael Dowling and Householder's strategist Jeff Longstreth. FirstEnergy pledged $1 million to Householder's run for Speaker. According to the appeals court's summary of the trial evidence, Dowling advised setting up a nonprofit that could take unlimited, undisclosed contributions, and Householder had Longstreth create one, Generation Now. The money arrived in four $250,000 payments over 2017.25 HouseholderJones

The same court opinion describes a meeting at a West Virginia resort in the summer of 2017, where Longstreth briefed Jones and Dowling on the Speaker's race. By the court's account, Jones said: "we have to get Larry in there . . . because I know he won't let anything bad happen to us."26 Jones and Dowling were not on trial in that case. Both have pleaded not guilty to charges of their own, and neither has been convicted.27

How the money hid

1

A nonprofit that does not name its donors

Generation Now registered as a social-welfare nonprofit, a 501(c)(4).28 Unlike a campaign or a PAC, a 501(c)(4) is not required to disclose who funds it, and there is no limit on how much a donor can give it.29 That is what "dark money" means, and it is legal.

2

Passed through more accounts to blur the trail

The money did not sit in one place. It moved through a chain of 501(c)(4)s, LLCs, and consulting firms before reaching candidates, ad buyers, operatives, and Householder himself.30 The $4.3 million for the regulator was paid to his consulting company under a consulting agreement.31

3

The crime was the exchange

Dark money by itself is legal. What made this a federal crime was the trade: prosecutors proved the payments were made in return for specific government action.32 The charge, racketeering conspiracy, means agreeing to run an organization through a pattern of crimes, in this case bribery and money laundering. The secrecy kept the public from seeing the trade while it was happening.

Part 2

What the money bought

HouseholderThe Speaker's chair. In 2018 the operation backed about 21 state House candidates, Householder among them. Most won,33 and in January 2019 Householder became Speaker of the Ohio House.34

House Bill 6. Signed in July 2019,35 HB6 created a ratepayer-funded subsidy of roughly $150 million a year, scheduled to run from 2021 through 2027, for two nuclear plants then owned by FirstEnergy's generation arm, added coal-plant subsidies, and cut Ohio's renewable-energy and efficiency standards.36 It also guaranteed FirstEnergy's three Ohio utilities a fixed level of yearly revenue no matter how much electricity customers used, through a "decoupling" charge on bills. The appeals court described this as a fixed revenue stream of $20 to $50 million a year through 2024.8 Lawmakers repealed it in 2021, and regulators ordered about $27.5 million in collected charges, with interest, refunded.37

Keeping it. When citizens tried to put a repeal on the ballot, the operation spent heavily to stop them, including ads that falsely claimed China was invading the electric grid38 and payments to block or buy off petition circulators.39 The repeal never reached the ballot.40

RandazzoThe regulator. On January 2, 2019, FirstEnergy wired $4.3 million to a consulting company owned by Sam Randazzo. His selection to lead PUCO was announced on February 4, and he took office as chair that April.45 The money was an early payout of a consulting agreement that ran through 2024. FirstEnergy later admitted that it had no legal obligation to pay at that time, and that it paid so he would take official action as PUCO chair to further the company's interests.46 One example from that admission: in November 2019, PUCO dropped a requirement that FirstEnergy's Ohio utilities, including The Illuminating Company, file a new rate case in 2024.47 A rate case is the review in which regulators examine a utility's spending and rates. Randazzo was never tried; see below.

Part 3

How it unraveled

HouseholderLongstrethCespedesOn July 21, 2020, the FBI arrested Householder and four associates on a federal racketeering conspiracy charge.9 The case became one of the largest public corruption cases in Ohio history.54 Generation Now itself later pleaded guilty,55 and agreed to forfeit its assets, including nearly $1.5 million seized from its bank accounts.52 Two associates, strategist Jeff Longstreth and FirstEnergy Solutions lobbyist Juan Cespedes, pleaded guilty to racketeering conspiracy in October 2020 and later testified for the government at Householder's trial.5657 Lobbyist Neil Clark died by suicide in 2021 before trial.58

In July 2021 FirstEnergy Corp. was charged with conspiracy to commit honest services wire fraud and signed a deferred-prosecution agreement with federal prosecutors: the government agreed to dismiss the charge later if the company admitted the facts, paid a penalty, and cooperated. In it the company admitted paying millions of dollars to public officials in return for official action, and it paid a $230 million penalty. Half went to the U.S. Treasury and half to an Ohio program that helps low-income customers pay their electric bills. None of it was refunded on ordinary bills.1159 It had already fired its CEO, Chuck Jones, and senior vice president Michael Dowling in 2020.60 In 2024 it paid another $100 million to settle Securities and Exchange Commission fraud charges.61

BorgesIn March 2023 a federal jury convicted Householder and the fourth arrested associate, lobbyist and former Ohio Republican Party chair Matt Borges, of racketeering conspiracy.53 Householder was sentenced to 20 years10 and Borges to five.62 Borges was moved from prison to a halfway house in October 2025 to serve the rest of his sentence.63 The Sixth Circuit upheld the convictions in May 2025, and the U.S. Supreme Court declined to hear the appeals in April 2026.64 Householder is seeking a presidential pardon.65 A presidential pardon applies only to federal crimes. Householder was also indicted in Cuyahoga County in March 2024 on 10 state felony counts, including theft in office and money laundering.66

Randazzo was indicted by federal and state prosecutors67 and died by suicide in April 2024, before any trial; the charges against him were dismissed.68 His consulting company, the Sustainability Funding Alliance of Ohio, pleaded guilty in the state case in Summit County that December and agreed to give up more than $2.1 million in fines, forfeited money, and restitution. State prosecutors called it a shell company.69

Part 4

Getting money back

The largest piece of the bailout, the nuclear subsidy, was never collected. Lawmakers repealed it, along with decoupling, in March 2021.12 PUCO ordered the decoupling charges refunded to customers.37 The coal-plant subsidy was different. Customers of every large Ohio utility, including CEI, paid it from January 2020 until House Bill 15, signed in May 2025 and in effect that August, ended it. The state's consumer advocate put the total at about half a billion dollars.13 That money went to the utilities that co-own the two coal plants, and it has not been refunded. HB6's cuts to Ohio's renewable-energy and efficiency standards were not reversed.72

Restitution. In November 2025 PUCO found that FirstEnergy's three Ohio utilities, including CEI, had violated Ohio law, PUCO rules, and PUCO orders,14 and ordered them to pay $250.7 million: about $186.6 million back to customers and about $64.1 million in civil forfeitures (penalties).73 A settlement PUCO approved in January 2026 built on that order and sent the money to customers rather than to the state as forfeitures: $249 million in bill credits, plus $20 million for low-income bill assistance and weatherization.74 The settlement took the place of the November payment terms; the two figures are not added together. The $249 million was credited over three consecutive monthly bills expected to begin in February 2026. The city put the total at about $65.61 for a customer using 1,000 kilowatt-hours a month.15

Rates. In a separate rate case decided the same day as the restitution order, November 19, 2025, PUCO cut FirstEnergy's requested $190 million a year distribution rate increase to about $34 million across its three Ohio utilities.75 On rehearing in February 2026 it cut a further $39.4 million a year, leaving the three utilities combined with a net decrease of about $5 million a year. The result was not even. Ohio Edison and Toledo Edison rates went down, by about $24.5 million and $29.5 million a year. CEI, Lakewood's utility, was allowed to collect about $48.7 million a year more, effective March 1, 2026.76 FirstEnergy's utilities appealed the case to the Ohio Supreme Court in August 2026 (case 2026-1056).77 The larger increase CEI is now asking for is a separate, newer case, described in Part 6.

Part 5

Meanwhile, Lakewood's grid

Lakewood's power is delivered by one company, The Illuminating Company (CEI), one of the three FirstEnergy Ohio utilities that PUCO ordered to pay restitution and forfeitures in 2025.1673 In two summers in a row its equipment failed in the heat, and both times PUCO Staff sent CEI a notice of probable non-compliance. The second, in August 2026, proposes a $3.05 million fine.517 This section follows the record in order: what was known before, what failed, and what the company and the state have said since.

A notice of probable non-compliance is PUCO Staff's conclusion after investigating. It is not a ruling by the commissioners. The 2025 notice ended with a Staff letter accepting corrective actions in place of a fine; the 2026 notice and its proposed fine are still pending.

How the two stories connect. No court or regulator has found that the HB6 scheme caused Lakewood's outages, and this page does not claim it did. The connection on the record is narrower. CEI is owned by FirstEnergy.16 PUCO regulates it, and FirstEnergy admitted paying $4.3 million to a company owned by the man who became PUCO's chair in 2019.7 From 2017 to 2019, FirstEnergy's Ohio customers paid a "distribution modernization" charge that the Ohio Supreme Court struck down because nothing required the money to be spent on the grid.22 Under that chair, PUCO dropped a requirement that FirstEnergy file a new rate case in 2024.47 PUCO is now deciding how fast Lakewood's equipment is replaced and who pays.19

Old and near capacity. PUCO Staff counts more than 30 circuits serving Lakewood and surrounding areas in whole or in part (at least 20 serve the city itself, by Staff's 2025 count), fed by substations including Lakewood, Lauderdale, Edgewater, Grovewood, and Freedom. After field visits, Staff described a significant amount of the equipment as seeming older, on the whole, than elsewhere in CEI's territory, and reported that many of the substations shift load to one another when the daily high passes 90°F because of capacity limits.2 CEI's own response describes its improvement plan as focused on reducing and retiring its 4 kV system in favor of the more standard 13.2 kV.78 Age is not the whole story. Two transformers at the Lakewood substation were installed in 2007, and in October 2025 Staff required CEI to investigate their "premature failure."17

Warnings before the failures. On September 13, 2024, PUCO Staff sent CEI an earlier notice of probable non-compliance, this one covering its whole territory rather than Lakewood: the average time to restore power to a customer who lost it was about 145 minutes in both 2022 and 2023, against a standard of 135. Staff accepted CEI's action plan and there was no fine.79 In December 2024 FirstEnergy asked PUCO to loosen its reliability standards.80 Through late 2024 and early 2025, repeated Lakewood outages traced to equipment at the Lakewood and Grovewood substations, and on February 19, 2025, CEI told Staff no large projects were planned there that year.4 A maintenance record CEI filed in its 2026 rate case shows four repair orders opened on Lakewood substation transformer No. 42 between December 11 and 20, 2024, and a fifth on June 2, 2025, three weeks before the summer failures.81

The second notice. On August 7, 2026, Staff sent CEI a second notice of probable non-compliance and proposed a $3,049,750 forfeiture (a fine), along with new corrective actions, including replacing by the end of 2027 any equipment that failed while running above its designed peak load, delivering a replacement schedule for all of Lakewood's distribution equipment by the end of 2026, and producing a plan to replace all the underground lead cables serving substations in and around Lakewood.5 CEI answered that its long-term strategy is "not to replace all existing infrastructure on a like-for-like basis" and promised Staff an overall plan by December 31, 2026. It said it "does not recommend replacing the underground lead cables between Clinton Substation and Lauderdale Substation due to the cost and time required to replace" (six circuits, about 34.5 miles of cable). It also recommended that the money for the proposed forfeiture be invested in the Lakewood system instead of going to the state treasury, where it said the money would "not directly benefit the customers who experienced the outages."78

At a Crain's Cleveland Business event in August 2026, FirstEnergy CEO Brian Tierney said of Lakewood's grid, "It's too old. It's not reliable. It's at capacity, and that system needs to be replaced," and said replacing it will take seven to nine years, according to the mayor's account of his remarks.18 The mayor wrote that CEI's August 21 written response to PUCO is less explicit about the scope of the problem and the time needed to fix it.18

What FirstEnergy says. In its August 21 response to Staff, CEI said its plan differs from the notice: Staff's corrective actions focus on "renewing the system in its current configuration," while CEI plans to retire the old 4 kV system in favor of 13.2 kV, so that it is not "simply replacing aging infrastructure with facilities of similar design."78

Part 6

What is being decided now

CEI's Three-Year Rate Plan (PUCO Case 26-0347-EL-AIR). This case decides how much CEI may collect and what it must build with the money. The City of Lakewood moved to intervene on August 20, 2026. The deadline to intervene passed on September 29, 2026; residents can still file public comments and testify at the local public hearings. The PUCO Staff Report is due November 30, 2026. The formal hearing, with lawyers and expert witnesses, begins March 1, 2027 in Columbus. Local public hearings, where any resident can speak, have not been scheduled yet. If PUCO has not ruled within 360 days of July 1, 2026, the date the application was found complete, the plan is deemed approved automatically under state law. That date is about June 26, 2027.19

What the filing says about Lakewood. Asked in his written testimony whether the plan addresses Lakewood, CEI's president, Torrence Hinton, answered yes: the outages were "primarily due to equipment failures," and in early 2025 CEI began "a comprehensive reliability improvement plan," including "replacing or repairing transformers and breakers." He referred the details to another witness, Mark Josef.92 Josef's testimony does not mention Lakewood. It describes company-wide spending categories, including $276.9 million for equipment failure or damage across CEI over three years, without locations.93 We searched the rest of the company's filing and found no Lakewood project, schedule, or dollar amount.94 Some of that money may well be spent in Lakewood; the filing does not say how much, or on what. The same Torrence Hinton signed the September 2026 letter to Lakewood households described above.91

The second Lakewood notice. Staff has the next move on three things: the changes CEI proposed to the corrective actions, its position against replacing the Clinton to Lauderdale lead cables, and its recommendation that the proposed $3,049,750 forfeiture be invested in its Lakewood system instead of paid to the state treasury. CEI's response does not say whether customers would later be charged for that spending through rates. As of the mayor's September 25 letter, PUCO was still weighing CEI's response.18 CEI has proposed its own dates. By October 31, 2026 it says it will inspect the accessible cable in the remaining 139 of 153 manholes on the Clinton to Lauderdale lines (Staff had asked for September 4), review the age and expected remaining life of the equipment serving Lakewood, and determine whether the equipment that failed this summer was overloaded. By December 31, 2026 it says it will give Staff an overall plan for what will be retired, replaced, or upgraded, and it says it is on track to complete the five corrective actions still open from 2025.78

What you can do

HB6 was passed with the help of money the public could not see. The decisions about Lakewood's grid are being made in proceedings that are public, and residents can take part in them. If you do one thing, do the first.
